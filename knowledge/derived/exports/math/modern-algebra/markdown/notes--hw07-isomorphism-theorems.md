# Homework 7

## 1. Cyclic groups and a sign isomorphism

Let $G$ and $H$ be groups.

\(a\) Give an example where $G$ and $H$ are both cyclic, but $G \times H$ is not.

\(b\) If $G \times H$ is a cyclic group, prove that $G$ and $H$ are both cyclic.

\(c\) Recall that ${\mathbb{R}}^{\times}$ is the multiplicative group of units of $\mathbb{R}$. Define an explicit isomorphism $f:{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}} \times {\mathbb{Z}}_{2}$.

### (a)

${\mathbb{Z}}_{2} = \left\langle 1_{2} \right\rangle$ is cyclic, but ${\mathbb{Z}}_{2} \times {\mathbb{Z}}_{2}$ is not cyclic. It cannot be generated by any element among its four elements, by either addition or multiplication.

### (b)

**Proof.** Let $\left( {g,h} \right)$ be the generating element such that

$$
\left\langle \left( {g,h} \right) \right\rangle = G \times H.
$$

Take arbitrary $m \in G$ and $n \in H$. Then $\left( {m,n} \right) = \left( {g,h} \right)^{k}$ for some integer $k$. Hence $m = g^{k}$ and $n = h^{k}$. Therefore $G = \left\langle g \right\rangle$ and $H = \left\langle h \right\rangle$ are cyclic groups.

### (c)

Let

$$
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times} = \left\{ ,a \in {\mathbb{R}}\  \middle| \ a \neq 0, \right\}.
$$

Define $\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}} \times {\mathbb{Z}}_{2}$ by

$$
\varphi(a) = \left( {\ln|a|,\varepsilon(a)} \right),
$$

Note that ${\mathbb{R}} \times {\mathbb{Z}}_{2}$ is an additive group, while ${\mathbb{R}}^{\times}$ is a multiplicative group.

Take arbitrary $a,b \in {\mathbb{R}}^{\times}$. Then

$$
\begin{matrix}
{\varphi(a) + \varphi(b)} & {= \left( {\ln|a|,m} \right) + \left( {\ln|b|,n} \right)} \\
 & {= \left( {\ln\left| {ab} \right|,m + n} \right)}
\end{matrix}
$$

where $\varepsilon(a) = 0_{2}$ if $a > 0$ and $\varepsilon(a) = 1_{2}$ if $a < 0$; likewise $m = \varepsilon(a)$ and $n = \varepsilon(b)$. Thus $m + n = 0_{2}$ if $\operatorname{sign}(m) = \operatorname{sign}(n)$, i.e. if $ab > 0$, and $m + n = 1_{2}$ if $\operatorname{sign}(m) \neq \operatorname{sign}(n)$, i.e. if $ab < 0$. Therefore

$$
\varphi(a) + \varphi(b) = \varphi\left( {ab} \right),
$$

so $\varphi$ is a homomorphism.

Assume $\varphi(a) = \varphi(b)$. Then

$$
\varphi\left( {a - b} \right) = \varphi(a) - \varphi(b) = \left( {0,0_{2}} \right).
$$

So $\ln|a| = \ln|b|$ and $\operatorname{sign}(a) = \operatorname{sign}(b)$, hence $a = b$. Thus $\varphi$ is injective.

Let $\left( {m,n} \right) \in {\mathbb{R}} \times {\mathbb{Z}}_{2}$ be arbitrary. Consider

$$
a = e^{m}\left( {- 1} \right)^{y},
$$

where $y = 0$ if $n = 0_{2}$ and $y = 1$ if $n = 1_{2}$. Then $\varphi(a) = \left( {m,n} \right)$, so $\varphi$ is surjective. Therefore $\varphi$ is an isomorphism.

## 2. The unit circle

Let $S^{1} \subset {\mathbb{C}}$ be the unit circle:

$$
S^{1} = \left\{ ,z \in {\mathbb{C}}\  \middle| \ |z| = 1, \right\}.
$$

\(a\) Prove that $S^{1}$ is a subgroup of ${\mathbb{C}}^{\times}$.

\(b\) For every positive integer $n$, find an element of order $n$ in $S^{1}$.

\(c\) Find an element of infinite order in $S^{1}$.

### (a)

**Proof.** As $\mathbb{C}$ is a field, every element except $0$ is a unit in $\mathbb{C}$. Since $0! \in S^{1}$ and $S^{1} \subset {\mathbb{C}}$, $S^{1} \subset {\mathbb{C}}^{\times}$. Therefore it suffices to show that $S^{1}$ contains the identity and that every element has its multiplicative inverse also in $S^{1}$.

The first statement is true because $|1| = 1$. For the second, consider $a + bi \in S^{1}$. Since $\sqrt{a^{2} + b^{2}} = 1$,

$$
\left( {a + bi} \right)\left( {a - bi} \right) = a^{2} + b^{2} = 1,
$$

so $a - bi$ is the multiplicative inverse of $a + bi$. Therefore $S^{1}$ is a subgroup of ${\mathbb{C}}^{\times}$.

### (b)

By Euler's formula,

$$
e^{2\pi i} = \cos\left( {2\pi} \right) + i\sin\left( {2\pi} \right) = 1,
$$

so $e^{2\pi i} \in S^{1}$ and is the identity. For arbitrary $n \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}$, consider

$$
e^{2\pi\frac{i}{n}} = \cos\left( {2\frac{\pi}{n}} \right) + i\sin\left( {2\frac{\pi}{n}} \right) \in S^{1}.
$$

Because

$$
\left( e^{2\pi\frac{i}{n}} \right)^{n} = e^{2\pi i} = 1,
$$

the order of $e^{2\pi\frac{i}{n}}$ is $n$.

### (c)

Consider

$$
e^{\sqrt{2}\pi i} = \cos\left( {\sqrt{2}\pi} \right) + i\sin\left( {\sqrt{2}\pi} \right).
$$

For every $n \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}$,

$$
\left( e^{\sqrt{2}\pi i} \right)^{n} = e^{\sqrt{2}\pi in} \neq e^{2\pi i},
$$

since $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational. Thus its order is infinite.

## 3. Units in matrix rings

Let $R$ be a commutative ring, and consider the group $\operatorname{GL}_{2}(R)$ of units in the ring of $2 \times 2$ matrices $M_{2}(R)$.

\(a\) Suppose $A = \begin{pmatrix}
a & c \\
b & d
\end{pmatrix} \in M_{2}(R)$ and all entries are in an ideal $I \subsetneq R$. Prove that $A$ is not a unit.

\(b\) Prove that for $A = \begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{pmatrix} \in M_{2}(R)$ there is a matrix $B$ such that $AB = BA = \det(A)I_{2}$.

\(c\) Prove that $A \in M_{2}(R)$ is a unit if and only if $\det(A)$ is a unit.

### (a)

**Proof.** Assume for sake of contradiction that $A$ is a unit. Then there is $B \in M_{2}(R)$ such that

$$
AB = BA = I_{2} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
$$

where $1$ is the multiplicative identity of $R$. Denote $B$ by $\begin{pmatrix}
m & n \\
p & q
\end{pmatrix}$. Then

$$
AB = \begin{pmatrix}
{am + bp} & {an + bq} \\
{cm + dp} & {cn + dq}
\end{pmatrix} = I_{2}.
$$

Since $a,b \in I$ and $m,p \in R$, $am,bp \in I$, hence $am + bp \in I$ because $I$ is closed under addition. Therefore $1 \in I$, so $I = R$, which contradicts $I \subsetneq R$. Thus $A$ is not a unit.

### (b)

For $A = \begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{pmatrix} \in M_{2}(R)$, consider

$$
B = \operatorname{adj}(A) = \begin{pmatrix}
d & {- b} \\
{- c} & a
\end{pmatrix} \in M_{2}(R).
$$

Then, since $R$ is commutative,

$$
\begin{matrix}
{AB = BA} & {= \begin{pmatrix}
{ad - bc} & {bc - ab} \\
{ab - ba} & {da - cb}
\end{pmatrix}} \\
 & {= \begin{pmatrix}
{ad - bc} & 0 \\
0 & {ad - bc}
\end{pmatrix}} \\
 & {= \left( {ad - bc} \right)I_{2} = \det(A)I_{2}.}
\end{matrix}
$$

### (c)

Since $R$ is a commutative ring, $\det\left( {AB} \right) = \det(A)\det(B)$ for $A,B \in M_{2}(R)$.

**Claim 1.** If $A$ is a unit in $M_{2}(R)$, then $\det(A)$ is a unit in $R$.

**Proof.** Assume $A = \begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{pmatrix} \in M_{2}(R)$ is a unit. Then there is $C = \begin{pmatrix}
m & n \\
p & q
\end{pmatrix} \in M_{2}(R)$ such that $AC = CA = I_{2}$. Hence

$$
\det(A)\det(C) = \det\left( I_{2} \right) = 1
$$

and also $\det(C)\det(A) = 1$ in $R$. Therefore $\det(A)$ is a unit in $R$ by definition.

**Claim 2.** If $\det(A)$ is a unit in $R$, then $A$ is a unit in $M_{2}(R)$.

**Proof.** Assume $\det(A)$ is a unit in $R$. Then there is $m \in R$ such that

$$
m\det(A) = \det(A)m = 1.
$$

By part (b), there is $B \in M_{2}(R)$ such that $BA = AB = \det(A)I_{2}$. Consider $C = \left( {\det(A)} \right)^{- 1}B = mB$. Then

$$
CA = AC = \left( {m\det(A)} \right)I_{2} = I_{2}.
$$

Therefore $A$ is a unit in $M_{2}(R)$. By Claims 1 and 2, $A$ is a unit in $M_{2}(R)$ if and only if $\det(A)$ is a unit in $R$.

## 4. Matrices over ${\mathbb{Z}}_{p}$

Let $p$ be a prime number and consider the field ${\mathbb{Z}}_{p}$.

\(a\) Show that a $2 \times 2$ matrix $A \in M_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ is not a unit if and only if the columns are linearly dependent.

\(b\) Show that the set of upper triangular invertible matrices in $\operatorname{GL}_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ forms a subgroup of order ${p\left( {p - 1} \right)}^{2}$, which is non-abelian when $p \neq 2$.

\(c\) Compute the order of $\operatorname{GL}_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$.

\(d\) Show that diagonal invertible matrices form an abelian subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ of order $\left( {p - 1} \right)^{2}$.

\(e\) Find an abelian subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ of order $p$.

### (a)

**Claim 1.** $A = \begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{pmatrix} \in M_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ is not a unit if the columns are linearly dependent.

**Proof.** Assume the columns of $A$ are linearly dependent. Then

$$
m\begin{pmatrix}
a \\
c
\end{pmatrix} + n\begin{pmatrix}
b \\
d
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
0 \\
0
\end{pmatrix}
$$

where $m,n \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{p}$ are not both $0$. Without loss of generality, assume $m \neq 0$. Since ${\mathbb{Z}}_{p}$ is a field, $m^{- 1} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{p}$, so

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
a \\
c
\end{pmatrix} = - m^{- 1}n\begin{pmatrix}
b \\
d
\end{pmatrix},
$$

which gives $\det(A) = ad - bc = 0$. This is not a unit. Since ${\mathbb{Z}}_{p}$ is a field and hence a commutative ring, problem 3 shows that $A \in M_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ is a unit if and only if $\det(A)$ is a unit in ${\mathbb{Z}}_{p}$. Thus $A$ is not a unit.

**Claim 2.** If $A \in M_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ is not a unit, then the columns are linearly dependent.

Assume $A$ is not a unit and the columns are not linearly dependent. Then $\det(A) \neq 0_{p}$. Let $\det(A) = m$. Consider

$$
B = m^{- 1}\begin{pmatrix}
d & {- b} \\
{- c} & a
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then $AB = BA = I_{2}$, contradicting that $A$ is not a unit. So the columns are linearly dependent. Therefore $A \in M_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ is not a unit if and only if the columns of $A$ are linearly dependent.

### (b)

The set of upper triangular invertible matrices in $\operatorname{GL}_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ is

$$
S = \left\{ ,\begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
0 & c
\end{pmatrix}\  \middle| \ a\text{,}b\text{,}c \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\ \text{and}\ a\text{,}c \neq 0_{p}, \right\}.
$$

Since $I_{2} \in S$, and for $A = \begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
0 & c
\end{pmatrix} \in S$ we have $a,c \neq 0$, take

$$
B = \left( {ac} \right)^{- 1}\begin{pmatrix}
c & {- b} \\
0 & a
\end{pmatrix} \in S.
$$

Then $AB = BA = I_{2}$, so every element in $S$ has its inverse also in $S$. Thus $S$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$.

There are $p - 1$ different choices for $a$, $p - 1$ different choices for $c$, and $p$ different choices for $b$. The three choices are independent, so there are ${p\left( {p - 1} \right)}^{2}$ elements in $S$.

Take $\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & {p - 1}
\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}$ in $S$. Then

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & {p - 1}
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 2 \\
0 & {p - 1}
\end{pmatrix},
$$

whereas

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & {p - 1}
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & p \\
0 & {p - 1}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Since these are different if $p \neq 2$, $S$ is non-abelian when $p \neq 2$.

When $p = 2$, consider arbitrary $\begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
0 & c
\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}
m & n \\
0 & p
\end{pmatrix} \in S$. Since $a,c,m,p \neq 0$, we have $a = c = m = p = 1$, and

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
0 & c
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}
m & n \\
0 & p
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
{am} & {an + bp} \\
0 & {cp}
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & {b + n} \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
m & n \\
0 & p
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
0 & c
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
{am} & {bm + cn} \\
0 & {cp}
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & {b + n} \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus $S$ is abelian if $p = 2$.

### (c)

By part (a), $A \in M_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ is a unit, i.e. $A \in \operatorname{GL}_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$, if and only if the columns of $A$ are linearly independent. First choose an arbitrary nonzero vector $\begin{pmatrix}
a \\
b
\end{pmatrix} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{2}$; there are $\left( {p^{2} - 1} \right)$ choices. Then choose a vector $v$ which is linearly independent with it, i.e. $v \notin \left\{ ,k\begin{pmatrix}
a \\
b
\end{pmatrix}\  \middle| \ k \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{p}, \right\}$. There are $\left( {p^{2} - p} \right)$ choices for the second column. Hence

$$
\left| {\operatorname{GL}_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)} \right| = \left( {p^{2} - 1} \right)\left( {p^{2} - p} \right).
$$

### (d)

The set of diagonal invertible matrices is

$$
S = \left\{ ,\begin{pmatrix}
a & 0 \\
0 & b
\end{pmatrix} \in M_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)\  \middle| \ a\text{,}b \neq 0, \right\}.
$$

There are $p - 1$ choices for $a$ and $p - 1$ choices for $b$, so $|S| = \left( {p - 1} \right)^{2}$. To show it is a subgroup, $I_{2} \in S$, and for $A = \begin{pmatrix}
a & 0 \\
0 & b
\end{pmatrix} \in S$ consider

$$
B = \left( {ab} \right)^{- 1}\begin{pmatrix}
b & 0 \\
0 & a
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then $BA = I_{2}$, so $A$ has an inverse. Therefore $S$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ whose order is $\left( {p - 1} \right)^{2}$. It is abelian because, for $A = \begin{pmatrix}
a & 0 \\
0 & b
\end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix}
c & 0 \\
0 & d
\end{pmatrix}$ in $S$,

$$
AB = BA = \begin{pmatrix}
{ac} & 0 \\
0 & {bd}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

### (e)

$\left\langle \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix} \right\rangle$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}_{2}\left( {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} \right)$ whose order is $p$. This is a subgroup guaranteed by generating a cyclic subgroup from an element in the group. Also,

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}^{n} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & n \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
$$

so $\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}^{p} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & p \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix} = I_{2}$, while $\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}^{n} \neq I_{2}$ for $1 \leq n \leq p - 1$. Hence its order is $p$.
